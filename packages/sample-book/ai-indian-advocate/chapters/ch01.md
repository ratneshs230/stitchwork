The first time a senior partner handed you a 600-page paper book in 2018 and said
*"give me a one-page note by morning,"* what they meant was: read through the
night, summarise by hand, accept that the next day is gone. In 2026 the same
ask is a six-minute interaction with a properly-prompted model — and the
ethical question that ate the legal profession for two years is whether you
should disclose the assistance to the partner, to the client, to the bench.
The {regulatory-cite}`jurisdiction=IN, current_as_of=2026-05, source_url=https://www.barcouncilofindia.org/standards-of-professional-conduct-and-etiquette, statute=BCI-Standards-of-Professional-Conduct`[Bar Council standards]
say nothing direct, but Rule 36 of the Standards of Professional Conduct
binds you to truthful representation of the work product — and that has not
changed since 1961.

This chapter sets the scaffolding: what an advocate can ethically delegate
to a model, what they must do by hand, and what the model can never see.

## What a model can read

For everything that follows, assume the model in question is **isolated** —
running on infrastructure where the prompt and the response are not retained,
where vendor training is opted out, and where the data residency is
demonstrable. Without that, nothing in this book applies; you are simply
leaking client confidence into a vendor's corpus.

"I treat the model like a brilliant intern who has signed an NDA but
has no memory between conversations — and who will, if I am sloppy, hallucinate a
case I will then have to explain to the bench. The discipline is not technical.
The discipline is to verify every citation before it leaves my chamber."
:::

:::{locale-variant}
:locales: [IN]

In India the working assumption should be that any cloud-hosted model
without an India region commits the data outside Indian borders. The
DPDP Act 2023 frames this as cross-border processing requiring consent
or a notified category exception. For a privileged communication, the
safer default is on-premise or India-region inference; almost everything
else is litigation risk waiting to happen.
:::

:::{locale-variant}
:locales: [US, UK, AU]

Outside India the calculus differs by jurisdiction. In the US, evidentiary
work usually tolerates cloud inference if the vendor's BAA / DPA is sound.
In the UK the SRA Technology and Innovation Guidance (2025) frames the
duty in terms of competence and supervision rather than residency.
:::

## A first prompt skeleton

Before drafting an extension, an advocate's first prompt to any model should
look approximately like this:

```
Role: You are assisting a {{jurisdiction}} advocate. You do not invent citations.
If you are unsure of a citation, you say "verification required."

Task: {{task}}

Materials: {{briefing-pack}}

Constraints:
1. Cite only authorities included in Materials.
2. Mark any inference not directly supported by Materials with [inference].
3. Output in the format requested, no preamble.
```

The single largest source of model misbehaviour in legal contexts is the
absence of the "no invented citations" clause. The second largest is asking
the model to be *helpful* without telling it where to stop.

## What stays in your hand

There are three things the model does not do for you and will not do for you,
in 2026 or 2030.

1. **The signature.** Whether the document is filed or not is yours.
2. **The witness.** Examination cannot be delegated; cross-examination
   especially cannot.
3. **The verification of every citation.** The model can draft the
   citation; it cannot make it true. That last step is the bar's
   non-delegable duty and it is also why the next chapter is on
   citation verification workflows.

The remainder of this book is operational — prompt patterns, redaction
checklists, model-output review templates, and a small library of
matter-specific scaffolds that earn their keep within the first week.
Read it with a pencil; argue with the prompts; bring your own footnotes.
